Today's edition has an interesting common thread: trust in digital systems. From AI-powered fake students to disinformation entering established news outlets, several stories illustrate how technology is challenging institutions that were designed around the assumption that people and information are what they appear to be.

And there's some fascinating astronomy and North Country weather news, too.

STORY 01

“Ghost students” are using AI to stay enrolled — and Congress wants to intervene

Fraudsters using stolen or fabricated identities are no longer necessarily disappearing after collecting financial aid; some now submit AI-assisted coursework to stay enrolled for multiple semesters. The House-passed No Aid for Ghost Students Act would write federal FAFSA fraud screening into law, while requiring colleges to verify flagged applicants before releasing aid. The systems-design dilemma is striking: screening could protect public funds while adding friction for legitimate students who already face barriers to accessing college.

Why this caught my attention: For SUNY and community colleges, this isn't simply a financial-aid problem. It intersects with identity management, LMS activity, accessibility and student persistence.

STORY 02

AI propaganda is escaping social feeds and entering legitimate newsrooms

OpenAI reported on October 8 that an Iran-origin operation used seven fictitious journalist identities to pitch stories about the U.S.–Iran conflict; nearly 100 articles were published or syndicated across roughly a dozen outlets worldwide. A separate Russia-origin network apparently recruited unsuspecting staff to operate a Latin American research organization, lending its messaging the credibility of genuine human work. The new danger is less about obviously fake viral posts than the laundering of manufactured narratives through editorial and institutional trust.

What's particularly unsettling is that these operations exploited existing human processes. AI didn't invent disinformation, but it made the production and maintenance of convincing false identities substantially easier.

STORY 03

The FBI seized two Chinese hacking platforms used against universities and critical infrastructure

U.S. authorities announced on October 8 that they had seized domains supporting MicroScan and FishHub, tools attributed to the China-based Integrity Technology Group and used by Flax Typhoon and related actors. A joint international advisory describes MicroScan as a Python-based platform with over 1,300 vulnerability-scanning scripts; FishHub supported remote access and data theft, including attacks on at least 20 Taiwanese universities. The technical lesson for higher-ed IT is how commodity vulnerability scanners, compromised IoT devices and familiar administrative utilities can be assembled into a durable espionage service.

The especially relevant detail for enterprise administrators is that the attackers weren't relying exclusively on exotic exploits. They combined familiar reconnaissance tools, stolen credentials, VPN utilities and persistence mechanisms into an operational ecosystem.

STORY 04

Webb traced the farthest known fast radio burst to a surprisingly tiny galaxy

Astronomers used the James Webb Space Telescope to identify the host of fast radio burst FRB 20240304B, emitted when the universe was only about three billion years old. Instead of a large galaxy, Webb found an actively star-forming dwarf galaxy roughly a thousand times less massive than expected, strengthening the case that at least some bursts arise from young magnetars rather than mergers of old stellar remnants. The burst also carries imprints of matter along its path, making it a remarkably useful probe of the otherwise difficult-to-see cosmic web.

I love the scientific implications here: researchers are using a mysterious, millisecond-scale radio flash to investigate structures spanning billions of light-years. It's almost like using a naturally occurring flashlight to illuminate invisible material between galaxies.

STORY 05

A potentially historic El Niño could make the North Country's winter anything but typical

Climate researchers are watching a potentially record-strength El Niño develop for the coming winter, and NCPR asked Cornell climatologist Samantha Borisoff what that could mean locally. Strong El Niño winters often bring fewer cold-air outbreaks and less lake-effect snow to northern New York, but shifting storm tracks can still fuel powerful nor'easters and occasional heavy snowfall. For the Adirondacks, this is a story about probabilities rather than a firm forecast — with real implications for road crews, winter recreation and local businesses.

There's an interesting historical comparison: the 1982–83 winter, associated with one of the strongest El Niño events on record, was also the least snowy winter since 1950 for Buffalo, Syracuse and Watertown. That doesn't mean we'll repeat it, but it's a reminder of how strongly global atmospheric systems can influence our local conditions.
