Today's edition explores a theme I think you'll appreciate: unexpected connections between systems. SUNY is becoming part of New York's emergency-response infrastructure, malware operators are hiding instructions in poetry, and economists are questioning whether AI could fundamentally change how governments collect taxes.

There's also an extraordinary discovery involving colliding neutron stars and some intriguing new cannabis research.

STORY 01

SUNY has quietly trained 568 students to become part of New York's disaster-response network

SUNY announced on October 8 that 568 Empire State Service Corps students have completed American Red Cross disaster-relief training, preparing them to be mobilized during emergencies anywhere in New York. They'll also support a statewide disaster-relief hotline, while the broader paid service program has expanded to 1,000 positions across 58 SUNY campuses. What's fascinating is that SUNY is effectively developing a distributed emergency-response network, using students and existing campus infrastructure to strengthen statewide resilience.

Why this matters: It's a terrific example of systems thinking applied to public institutions. Individual campuses gain a new role as interconnected components of a larger civic-support system, rather than operating solely as educational institutions.

STORY 02

A cryptomining botnet is receiving instructions from a poem hosted on GitHub

Lumen's Black Lotus Labs has uncovered an unusually creative malware campaign called Canto Incognito, which has compromised more than 3,400 servers, particularly those running exposed AI and developer services such as LiteLLM, Ollama, Gotenberg, and Gitea. Its malware, PoeLLM, reads a two-stanza poem stored in a GitHub repository and decodes four particular words to determine where its command-and-control server is located; the attackers have modified the poem 11 times to redirect infected machines. Compromised systems are used for cryptocurrency mining and scanning for additional vulnerable servers.

The enterprise IT connection: This is a clever example of hiding malicious infrastructure information inside completely ordinary web content. The attack also highlights the risks of deploying self-hosted AI services without appropriate network restrictions, patch management, and monitoring.

STORY 03

The IMF says AI could force governments to rethink how they collect taxes

Speaking at the IMF–World Bank meetings in Bangkok on October 12, IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva warned that AI could transform approximately 40% of jobs worldwide, rising to 60% in advanced economies, through a combination of augmentation, reduced demand, and elimination. She also noted that AI infrastructure investment is helping sustain global economic growth around 3%, even as the technology threatens to shift economic activity from labor toward capital. That raises an intriguing structural problem: governments rely heavily on taxing employment, but what happens when a growing share of economic output comes from machines and infrastructure rather than workers?

Singapore's president raised a related concern about AI reducing opportunities for new graduates entering the workforce.

The systems-thinking angle: This isn't merely an employment problem. It's a potential feedback loop involving education, taxation, income distribution, government services, and economic inequality.

STORY 04

A half-second cosmic flash concealed nearly ten minutes of activity from colliding neutron stars

Researchers analyzing observations from the Einstein Probe spacecraft discovered that an extremely brief gamma-ray burst, lasting less than half a second, was accompanied by approximately 560 seconds of soft X-ray emission. The event, originally detected in July 2025 and described in research highlighted on October 8, appears to have resulted from a merger involving compact stellar objects, potentially leaving behind a rapidly rotating, highly magnetized neutron star called a magnetar. Scientists believe similar extended emissions may accompany other neutron-star mergers but have gone undetected because previous instruments weren't sensitive to this particular form of radiation.

Here's what I find remarkable: We may have been observing these incredibly violent cosmic events for decades while missing most of what happened immediately afterward.

Better detection of these extended X-ray signatures could also help astronomers identify counterparts to gravitational-wave events, giving them a much richer picture of the physics involved.

STORY 05

A new cannabis study reveals surprisingly similar patterns among rural and urban cancer patients

Researchers at Penn State Cancer Institute published a peer-reviewed study on October 9 examining cannabis use among 186 cancer patients, finding that approximately 26% reported using cannabis after diagnosis, with no statistically significant difference between rural and urban/suburban participants. Among respondents with prior cannabis experience who answered the relevant follow-up questions, about 30% reported using cannabis instead of opioids for pain, while many users described perceived improvements in sleep, appetite, and other symptoms. Importantly, these findings reflect patients' self-reported experiences, not controlled evidence that cannabis is effective or a safe substitute for prescribed medications.

Why this is interesting: Rural populations are frequently underrepresented in cannabis research, making the comparison particularly valuable.

The researchers also found differences in how participants obtained cannabis and perceived medication interactions. They argue that larger, more rigorous studies are needed to determine actual therapeutic effects and safety.
