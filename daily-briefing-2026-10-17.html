Today's edition has a theme: understanding when and why systems change.

We've got a SUNY project reshaping a North Country community, a particularly exciting development in AI-based tipping-point detection, a cybersecurity incident involving a spectacular failure of automated alert handling, a new atlas of Martian soils, and fascinating research into how our brains' natural cannabinoid systems change with age.

STORY 01

SUNY Canton is helping transform a long-abandoned shopping plaza into a $27 million community hub

Local officials celebrated the demolition of Canton's Midtown Plaza on October 15, making way for a planned $27 million redevelopment that includes 60 apartments, retail space, and a SUNY Canton-operated entrepreneurship center. The property has been largely vacant since the Jubilee supermarket closed more than two decades ago, and the project brings together state funding, private development, and university resources. Construction timing hasn't been finalized, but the development represents something bigger than urban renewal: a public university becoming part of the infrastructure supporting housing, entrepreneurship, and economic development in its surrounding community.

Why this caught my attention: Higher education's contribution to a community isn't limited to graduates and degrees. SUNY Canton is positioning itself as an active participant in the economic and social systems surrounding it.

STORY 02

Caltech researchers developed an AI that can predict when complex systems reach a tipping point

In research announced October 15, scientists at Caltech demonstrated that a recurrent neural operator (RNO) could anticipate abrupt transitions in simplified climate and aerodynamic models. In one simulated climate scenario involving the sudden disappearance of low-altitude clouds, the model successfully predicted the tipping point more than two years in advance, with an error of approximately 11 days. Unlike conventional forecasting methods that assume relatively stable underlying dynamics, this approach incorporates memory of how a system's behavior changes over time; it remains experimental and has not yet demonstrated comparable accuracy for real-world climate tipping events.

Here's what makes this particularly fascinating: This research deals directly with nonstationary complex systems.

The researchers aren't merely trying to predict a future state using historical patterns. They're developing methods to detect when the relationships governing a system are changing.

For Systems Science research, that's an important distinction, especially when investigating early warning signals, resilience, and system collapse.

STORY 03

A critical BeyondTrust vulnerability reveals an even more troubling problem: automated security alerts were ignored

BeyondTrust disclosed CVE-2026-102764 on October 15, a critical vulnerability affecting its Remote Support platform that a suspected China-linked espionage group exploited as a zero-day beginning August 29. The attackers reportedly compromised remote-support appliances, established persistence, accessed stored credentials, and removed evidence of their activity. Perhaps most concerning, BeyondTrust acknowledges that its security sensors generated alerts during an intrusion, but a third-party automated triage system incorrectly dismissed them as false positives; self-hosted customers are being urged to apply the BT26-05-RS patch and investigate external logs for possible compromise.

The enterprise IT lesson: This is a fascinating example of how automation can simultaneously strengthen and weaken security.

A detection system technically worked, but the process deciding whether humans should see its alerts failed. It demonstrates why automated security decisions need appropriate oversight and why retaining logs outside potentially compromised systems matters.

Censys has also published a geographic visualization of internet-exposed BeyondTrust installations, although those numbers do not establish how many installations remain vulnerable.

STORY 04

A doctoral student has classified nearly 800 million locations on Mars into 2,000 soil categories

A University of Sydney PhD student has created what researchers describe as the first comprehensive classification system for Martian soils, announced October 16. Using orbital observations and computational models, Daniel Irving classified nearly 800 million locations into 2,000 soil categories called areths, identifying one category that scored particularly well for properties associated with agricultural potential. The system could eventually help scientists identify promising locations for future exploration and controlled food production, although the models still lack important information about soil toxicity, nutrients, and other conditions necessary for growing crops.

Here's the wonderful scientific connection: We're beginning to study Mars using some of the same classification techniques agricultural scientists employ on Earth.

Instead of examining isolated landing sites, researchers can now compare soil characteristics systematically across nearly the entire planet.

I also love that the work came from a doctoral student. It's a reminder of how much original research can emerge from applying familiar scientific methods to entirely new environments.

STORY 05

A new neuroscience review suggests the brain's natural cannabinoid system changes substantially as we age

A review published in Nature Reviews Neuroscience on October 16 examines how the brain's endocannabinoid system changes throughout life, with signaling activity generally declining as we age. The researchers highlight evidence connecting this decline to changes in cognitive function, neural plasticity, and inflammation, alongside animal experiments suggesting that carefully controlled low-dose THC exposure may counteract certain age-related changes. Importantly, these findings do not establish that cannabis improves memory or prevents cognitive decline in humans; rigorous clinical evidence remains limited.

What makes this interesting: We typically discuss cannabis in terms of external substances interacting with cannabinoid receptors.

But those receptors are components of a much larger biological signaling system that already exists within our bodies.

Understanding how that system changes over time could reveal new therapeutic possibilities, even if the eventual treatments look quite different from conventional cannabis products.
